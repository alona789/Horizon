## [SemiAnalysis Publishes Free Teardown of Intel 18A and Panther Lake](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/intel-panther-lake-teardown) ⭐️ 8.0/10

SemiAnalysis released a free teardown analysis of Intel&\#x27;s Panther Lake processor and the Intel 18A process node through its STEEL \(Teardown Engineering &amp; Evaluation Lab\) initiative, examining the chip&\#x27;s internal design and manufacturing. The piece is published as an open &quot;STEEL&quot; teardown article on the SemiAnalysis newsletter, with the deep technical detail gated behind a teaser for free readers. Independent teardowns of Intel 18A matter because 18A is the node Intel is betting on to regain process leadership and to win external foundry customers, so any evidence about its real density, power delivery and yield carries weight. Panther Lake is the first product built on 18A, making it the clearest public test case for whether Intel&\#x27;s advanced-node roadmap is on track. The analysis touches on technologies such as BSPD \(backside power delivery\) and GAAFET \(gate-all-around transistors\) as covered in SemiAnalysis&\#x27;s teardown framing, alongside die-shot level inspection of the physical layout. Context worth noting: Panther Lake is marketed as Intel Core Ultra Series 3 and is slated to power Windows laptops in 2026, while Intel&\#x27;s 18A family also includes 18A-P, which Intel claims delivers up to 9% better performance-per-watt.

rss · Semianalysis · Sep 26, 13:36

**Background**: Intel 18A is Intel&\#x27;s most advanced chip manufacturing node, named roughly for a 1.8nm-class generation, and it is the first node to combine RibbonFET gate-all-around transistors with PowerVia backside power delivery. A process node is the underlying fabrication recipe that determines how small, fast and power-efficient a chip can be, so node leadership directly affects product competitiveness. Panther Lake, announced by Intel on October 9, 2025, is the first product family built on 18A. STEEL is SemiAnalysis&\#x27;s dedicated chip teardown lab, based in Hillsboro, Oregon, that uses die shots and reverse engineering to evaluate advanced-node hardware, positioning itself alongside established firms like TechInsights.
